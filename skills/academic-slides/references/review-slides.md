# Review and survey talks

## 1. Define scope and organization

- Establish the topic, audience, purpose, and coverage boundary. Support teaching, field introductions, progress reports, method comparisons, or debate maps; do not assume a proposal defense.
- Organize by research questions, approaches, mechanisms, tasks, or disputes. Use chronology only when it explains a meaningful change; do not default to paper-by-paper narration.
- Do not require a traditional-methods → new-methods → gaps → personal-proposal sequence. Retain an accepted structure and choose background depth for this audience, without a universal background-page cap.
- Keep ordinary narrative overviews distinct from systematic or scoping reviews. Use reproducible search, screening, and deduplication methods appropriate to the promised coverage; do not impose a systematic-review protocol on every slide deck or claim exhaustiveness from an exploratory search.

## 2. Select representative evidence and compare fairly

- Use representative papers to illustrate approaches, comparisons, extensions, and boundaries; do not let three examples stand for an entire field.
- Record why each paper belongs to a theme. Sources need not test one hypothesis or use one causal mechanism.
- State comparison dimensions before constructing tables. Keep visible columns useful, such as study, verified venue/year, core method, and relevant evidence; keep full experimental conditions and interpretation limits in notes and the audit.
- Preserve differences in tasks, mechanisms, metrics, and validation. Do not turn heterogeneous findings into an unsupported league table.
- Apply the global publication-status checks: distinguish the published venue from the actual source version read, and distinguish main tracks, Findings, Industry, Workshops, journals, and preprints.

## 3. Synthesize progress, disagreement, and limits

For each theme, answer the applicable questions:

1. What problem does this line of work address?
2. Which approaches or capabilities have been established?
3. How do the approaches differ, and what evidence supports them?
4. Which conclusions are shared, disputed, or conditional?
5. If relevant, what remains uncovered, unseparated, or unvalidated?

- Make the summary do a job: synthesize a theme, explain a contrast, state progress plus a bounded limitation, or transition to the next section. Do not merely repeat the paper list.
- When a progress-and-limit summary is appropriate, use a supported form such as “Research has incorporated X into Y, but Z remains insufficiently compared or validated under conditions C.” Do not force this sentence pattern onto every page.
- Distinguish missing capability, missing controlled comparisons, and missing external validation. Not having isolated a mechanism's contribution does not imply having no such mechanism.
- Keep scope explicit: “the listed experiments did not compare X” is different from “no research addresses X.” Check close neighboring approaches before making absence or novelty claims; report search limits and unresolved evidence honestly.
- Do not dismiss older methods, static descriptions, or task-based systems to motivate a newer approach. Preserve capabilities they already support; distinguish richer representation from demonstrated realism or effectiveness.

## 4. Connect sections without manufacturing a proposal

- Use transitions to explain why the next theme follows, not to invent an unsupported deficiency in the previous theme.
- Close with the state of evidence, methodological tradeoffs, consensus, debates, or open questions as the talk requires. A review need not end with a research gap or the presenter's own project.
- If personal work is included, label it as a separate optional section and distinguish completed results, current work, and proposed experiments. Do not imply proposed benefits have been demonstrated.
- At the review acceptance gate, check thematic coverage against the stated scope, fair attribution, qualified gap language, and whether each synthesis follows from its sources. Apply the same global figure-readability and artifact checks as in the other modes.
